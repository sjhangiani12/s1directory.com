The Box Beside Every Register

Priced April 29, 2005

If you swiped a card at a shop counter in the 2000s, there was a good chance a VeriFone terminal read it. The company began in Honolulu in 1981 as a maker of check-verification devices, spent several years inside Hewlett-Packard, and came back out as an independent business backed by private equity. By the spring of 2005, chip cards were spreading across Europe and Asia, and every merchant on earth needed hardware that could keep up with the networks.

The Original Bet

The bet was simple: the moment a customer pays should be fast, cheap and trusted. Early VeriFone devices replaced the phone call a clerk made to check a card. Later models added PIN pads, receipt printers and secure software. VeriFone sold through banks and payment processors as well as directly, so its terminals spread far beyond any one retailer. The quiet strength of the business was its installed base, which turned each upgrade cycle into new orders.

Back on the Board

VeriFone priced its offering at $10 a share in April 2005 and listed on the New York Stock Exchange under the fitting ticker PAY. It grew through acquisitions, most notably the purchase of Lipman Electronic Engineering in 2006, and later pushed into taxi screens and mobile payments. Competition from newer, phone-based readers squeezed the old terminal business. In 2018 Francisco Partners took the company private in a deal worth about $3.4 billion.
