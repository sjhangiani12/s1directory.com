Riding the Edge of the Wave

Priced October 29, 1999

In the late 1990s, popular websites often slowed to a crawl or crashed under heavy traffic. Akamai, named for the Hawaiian word for smart, offered a fix born at MIT: copy content to thousands of servers around the world and serve each visitor from the nearest one. When it filed to go public in late 1999, the World Wide Wait was a real complaint, and Akamai had a mathematical answer.

The Original Bet

MIT professor Tom Leighton and graduate student Danny Lewin bet that clever algorithms could solve internet congestion without rebuilding the internet. Their system decided, in real time, which server should answer each request. Instead of one origin straining under demand, the load spread across a global network placed inside many internet providers, close to the people asking for content. It was an academic idea turned into a business.

The Wave and After

Akamai priced at $26 a share in October 1999 and closed its first day above $145, one of the great debuts of the era. The crash cut its value sharply, and Danny Lewin was killed aboard American Airlines Flight 11 on September 11, 2001. The company survived, grew into a core part of the internet's infrastructure and security, and still trades on Nasdaq.
