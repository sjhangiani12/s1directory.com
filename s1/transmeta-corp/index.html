A Chip That Changed Its Mind

Priced November 06, 2000

Transmeta worked in near-total secrecy for five years, and its hiring of Linux creator Linus Torvalds only added to the mystery. When it revealed the Crusoe processor in 2000, the idea was striking: a simple, power-saving chip that used software to act like an Intel processor. It promised thin laptops with long battery life. The company went public in November 2000, one of the last big hardware offerings of the boom.

The Original Bet

Transmeta bet that it could beat Intel on power use by moving complexity from hardware into software. Its code morphing layer translated programs as they ran, which let the hardware stay small and cool. Some Japanese laptop makers adopted Crusoe. Performance, though, lagged behind expectations, and Intel answered with its own power-saving chips. Its approach was technically bold, and engineers still study it.

Licensing the Ideas

Transmeta priced its IPO at $21 a share in November 2000 and listed on Nasdaq. The shares rose sharply on the first day, then fell for years as chip sales stayed small. The company shifted toward licensing its power-saving technology, and it won a large patent settlement from Intel. In 2009 Novafora acquired Transmeta. Its ideas about saving power, though, found their way into the wider industry.
