When Banks Compete for You

Priced February 15, 2000

Getting a mortgage in the 1990s meant visiting banks one by one and hoping the first offer was fair. LendingTree turned that around. A borrower filled out one form, and lenders bid for the loan. When the company filed to go public in early 2000, the idea of a consumer shopping lenders from a kitchen table was new, and the slogan that came to define it was already taking shape.

The Original Bet

Doug Lebda founded the company in 1996 in Charlotte, a banking city. His bet was that the internet could flip the loan process so that lenders competed for borrowers instead of the other way around. LendingTree did not lend money itself. It earned fees from lenders for qualified requests, which let it grow without holding loans on its own balance sheet.

Branches of the Tree

LendingTree priced at $12 a share in February 2000. It grew through the housing boom, and in 2003 IAC bought the company. IAC spun it back out as a separate public company in 2008, during the financial crisis, and Lebda returned to lead it. The business expanded from mortgages into credit cards, personal loans and insurance, and it still trades on Nasdaq under its original ticker.
