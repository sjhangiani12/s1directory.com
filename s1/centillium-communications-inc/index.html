Broadband Down the Old Wires

Priced May 23, 2000

In 2000, most homes reached the internet through a dial-up modem. Telephone companies wanted to sell faster service without laying new cable, and DSL let them push broadband down the copper lines already running to every house. Centillium, based in Fremont, California, designed the chips that made DSL equipment work. It went public in May 2000, when the race to wire homes for broadband was at full speed.

The Original Bet

Centillium bet that DSL would be the main way households got broadband, and that phone companies would buy huge amounts of equipment to offer it. Its chips went into the gear at the phone company's office and into some home modems. Large contracts in Japan, where DSL spread quickly, became a major source of revenue. It competed against much larger chip companies for these contracts.

Slower Lines

Centillium priced its IPO at $19 a share in May 2000 and listed on Nasdaq. Its shares soared at first, then fell as telecom spending dropped and chip prices came down. The company tried to move into new areas such as voice-over-IP and fiber chips. In 2008 TranSwitch acquired Centillium. DSL itself went on to connect many millions of homes around the world.
