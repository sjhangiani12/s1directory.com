The Market After Hours

Priced May 17, 2001

Instinet was electronic trading before most people had heard of it. Founded in 1969, it let big institutions trade stocks with each other on computer terminals, without a floor broker. Reuters bought it in 1987. By the late 1990s Instinet and newer rivals were taking a large share of Nasdaq trading. In May 2001 Reuters sold part of Instinet to the public, and a year later Instinet bought Island, an upstart rival.

The Original Bet

Instinet bet that buyers and sellers could find each other directly through a screen and save on costs. It offered anonymity, which mattered to large investors who did not want to tip off the market. It also let institutions trade outside normal exchange hours. The model shaped what later became the standard for modern equity markets. Many of today's trading venues follow the path it opened.

Absorbed by the Exchange

Instinet priced its IPO at $14.50 a share in May 2001 and listed on Nasdaq. Competition from other electronic networks and falling trading fees squeezed it. In 2005 Nasdaq agreed to buy Instinet Group for about $1.9 billion, keeping its electronic marketplace. The Instinet brokerage arm was sold to the private equity firm Silver Lake and later to Nomura. Its offering came during a long shift from trading floors to computers.
