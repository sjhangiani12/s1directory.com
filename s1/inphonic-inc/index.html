A Free Phone in the Mail

Priced November 16, 2004

In 2004 Americans were trading up to camera phones and comparing plans from a crowded field of wireless carriers. InPhonic, based in Washington, D.C., ran websites where shoppers could pick a phone and plan side by side, often getting the handset free or nearly free. Carriers paid InPhonic a commission for every new customer. The company went public in November 2004, as online shopping for big purchases was becoming normal.

The Original Bet

InPhonic bet that buying a phone plan, a confusing purchase full of fine print, would move online. It ran its own sites and powered stores for other brands, then used carrier commissions to subsidize cheap handsets. The model grew fast. It also depended on rebates and on customers staying with their carriers long enough for commissions to hold. Its sites became some of the largest online sellers of wireless plans in the country.

Rebates and Reckoning

InPhonic priced its IPO at $19 a share in November 2004 and listed on Nasdaq. Revenue grew quickly, but losses mounted and customers complained about slow and missing rebates. Carrier payments were clawed back when subscribers cancelled early. In November 2007 InPhonic filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and its assets were sold to a new owner soon after. Its rise and fall showed how hard it was to profit from rebates.
