Routers Built for the Flood

Priced June 24, 1999

By 1999 internet traffic was doubling at a pace that strained the routers at its core. Those machines came almost entirely from Cisco, and many were general-purpose computers asked to do a very specialized job. Juniper offered a router built from the ground up for the backbone, with custom chips that forwarded packets at full line speed. Its first product had shipped only months before the company went public, and carriers were already lining up to test it.

The Original Bet

Pradeep Sindhu, a computer scientist from Xerox PARC, founded Juniper in 1996. His bet was that the internet core needed purpose-built hardware, not software running on general processors. Juniper split the router into two parts: a control plane for routing decisions and a forwarding engine in silicon that moved packets. Its M40 router, released in 1998, handled far more traffic per box than competitors and won early customers among large carriers.

After the Offering

Juniper priced its offering at $34 a share in June 1999, and it became one of the most successful offerings of the era. The stock soared through 2000 as carriers raced to build capacity, then fell sharply when the telecom bubble burst. Juniper survived as Cisco's main rival in carrier and data center networking for a quarter century. Hewlett Packard Enterprise completed its purchase of Juniper in 2025 for about $14 billion.
