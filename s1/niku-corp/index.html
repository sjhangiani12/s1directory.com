Scaffolding for Knowledge Work

Priced February 28, 2000

Niku sold software for a kind of work that factories had long measured and offices mostly had not: projects. Consulting firms, IT departments and service businesses ran on people's hours, but most tracked them in spreadsheets. When Niku filed to go public in early 2000, enterprise software was booming, and the idea of a single system to plan, staff and bill knowledge work sounded like the next big category.

The Original Bet

Farzad Dibachi co-founded Niku in 1998 after selling his earlier company, Diba, to Sun Microsystems. The bet was that service organizations needed their own version of the resource-planning systems that manufacturers already used. If a firm could see every project, every person and every hour in one place, it could price work better, staff it faster, and stop losing money on jobs it had already won.

Building Through the Bust

Niku priced at $24 a share in February 2000 and traded far higher in its first months. The crash that followed hit the company hard, and it spent several years cutting costs and narrowing its focus to IT governance and project portfolio software. The narrower product found steady buyers. In 2005 Computer Associates agreed to buy Niku for $21 a share in cash, about $350 million.
