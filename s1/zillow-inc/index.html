Every House Has a Number

Priced July 19, 2011

Zillow opened the front door of the American housing market. Its site showed listings, sales histories and a computed value, the Zestimate, for tens of millions of homes, including ones not for sale. When it filed to go public in 2011, housing was still deep in the wreckage of the financial crisis. Yet people kept looking, often at their own homes and their neighbors', and Zillow had become one of the most visited real estate sites in the country.

The Original Bet

Rich Barton had already turned travel booking into a consumer product at Expedia. With Lloyd Frink he bet that real estate data, long held close by agents and listing services, could be opened up to everyone. The Zestimate, launched in 2006, was the hook: a free, instant and sometimes argued-over number for any address. Zillow made its money from agents and advertisers who wanted to reach all those curious browsers.

The Road to the Bell

Zillow priced its IPO at $20 a share in July 2011 and traded well above that on its first day. It bought rival Trulia in 2015 and became the dominant home search site in the United States. A move into buying and flipping homes directly, Zillow Offers, ended in 2021 with large losses. The company returned to its roots as a marketplace and remains publicly traded.
